Chapter 1: Introduction to Numerical Bayes

There are two major ideologies that dominate modern statistics: Frequentist (Classical) and Bayesian statistics. Throughout this chapter, we will discuss how Thomas Bayes' perspective of statistics contrasts the assumptions and interpretations of the more commonly taught Frequestist approach to statistics.

First, we recall the structure of Frequentist inference. Suppose there exists some unknown true parameter vector \( \theta^* \in \Theta \) that explains the relationship between a random variable \( X \) that follows some distribution \( F \). Let \( x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \mathcal{X} \) denote the observed data generated from \( X \) with sample size \( n \).

\[ X \sim F(\theta^*), \quad x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \mathcal{X} \]

Frequentist inference quantifies the probability of observing a sample of data as extreme or more extreme than \( x \) under a hypothesized distribution \( F \). That is, for some hypothesized parameter \( \theta_0 \), we are interested in the frequency of obtaining a sample as extreme as our observed data over many repeated samples. This is the inspiration for the name "Frequentist," and it helps us obtain a confidence interval for \( \theta \) that we believe to contain the true population parameter \( \theta^* \) with some confidence level (e.g., 95%).

Without diving too deeply into Frequentist inference, we identify some qualities that raise concerns in application. Firstly, Frequentist ideology suggests the existence of some true constant parameter \( \theta^* \) that objectively describes the distribution of the data. Secondly, Frequentist inference relies on the notion of confidence intervals, which lack axiomatic interpretations in probability calculus. Additionally, the Frequentist approach reduces our analysis to a cross-sectional snapshot of an unknown fixed parameter. Bayesian inference addresses these conccerns by treating the parameter as a random variable that is informed by the observed data as opposed to the parameter being an unknown constant that inspires values taken by a random variable. Bayesians make claims about the distribution of the parameter itself that can be directly derived from the axioms of probability. Furthermore, we will discuss how iterative learning properties can help capture adaptations to our inference that have intuitive extensions to temporal data. This branch of ideology follows the structure of Thomas Bayes' concept of a posterior distribution that is dependent on the likelihood of the observed data and some prior distribution that encodes our beliefs about the parameter before observing the data. A priori information can be sourced from previous studies, expert knowledge, or even subjective beliefs. The relationship is described by Bayes' theorem, which states that the posterior distribution is proportional to the product of the likelihood distribution \( L(x | \theta) \) and the prior distribution \( p(\theta) \).

\[ {\color{purple}\text{Posterior}} = \frac{{\color{blue}\text{Likelihood}} \times {\color{red}\text{Prior}}}{{\color{green}\text{Marginal}}} \] \[ \begin{align} {\color{purple}p(\theta | x)} &= \frac{{\color{blue}L(x | \theta)} {\color{red}p(\theta)}}{{\color{green}p(x)}}, \quad \text{where } L(x | \theta) = \Pi_{i=1}^n f(x_i | \theta) \nonumber \\ &= \frac{{\color{blue}L(x | \theta)} {\color{red}p(\theta)}}{{\color{green}\int L(x | \theta) p(\theta) \text d\theta}}, \quad \text{(X, continuous random variable)} \\ &= \frac{{\color{blue}L(x | \theta)} {\color{red}p(\theta)}}{{\color{green}\sum L(x | \theta) p(\theta)}}, \quad \text{(X, discrete random variable)} \end{align} \] The above relationship includes a normalizing constant \( p(x) \) that ensures the posterior distribution integrates to 1, making it a valid probability distribution. We often call this the marginal (likelihood) distribution.

The goal of Bayesian inference is to obtain a credible interval \( C(x) \) such that \( P(\theta \in C(x) | x) = 1 - \alpha \) under the assumed model, where \( \alpha \) is the desired level of credibility (e.g., 0.05 for a 95% credible interval). Although different choices for the prior distribution can yield different credible intervals, posterior distributions with varying priors converge as sample size increases, which is a desirable property known as asymptotic consistency. Bayesian inference invites interative learning to the statistical model by replacing the prior distribution with the most recent posterior distribution. Consequently, we obtain an adaptive model that reflects our beliefs as new data affirms or contradicts our existing model. A continuously updating model intuitively captures the learning process of the parameter as a random variable that can adapt over time, contrasting the Frequentist interpretation of a cross-sectional snapshot of a fixed parameter.
