"A Student's Guide to Numerical Bayes" is a resource that I am currently developing based on "Computational Bayesian Statistics -- An Introduction" by M.A. Amaral Turkman, C.D. Paulino, and P. Mueller. It is my attempt to simplify to others (and to myself) the concepts and methods of numerical solutions to Bayesian inference in a more accessible and intuitive way. My goal is to provide timely examples that develop concepts gradually and to reveal step-by-step solutions to examples from the textbook. This guide is a work in progress and likely contains mistakes; please contact me to report any errors or to provide feedback.

Chapter 1: Introduction to Numerical Bayes

There are two major ideologies that dominate modern statistics: Frequentist (Classical) statistics and Bayesian statistics. Throughout this chapter, we will discuss how Thomas Bayes' perspective of statistics contrasts the assumptions and interpretations of more the commonly taught Frequestist approach to statistics.

First, we recall the structure of Frequentist inference. Suppose there exists some unknown true parameter vector (\ \theta^* \) that explains the relationship between some distribution and a random variable \( X \) that follows some distribution \( F \). Let \( x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \mathscr{X} \) denote the observed data generated from \( X \) with sample size \( n \).

\[ X \sim F(\theta^*), \quad x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \mathscr{X} \]

Frequentist inference quantifies the probability of observing sample data as extreme or more extreme than \( x \) under a hypothesized distribution \( F \). That is, for some hypothesized parameter \( \theta_0 \), we are interested in the frequency of obtaining a sample as extreme as our observed data over many repeated samples. This is the inspiration for the name "Frequentist," and it helps us obtain a confidence interval for \( \theta \) that we believe to contain the true population parameter \( \theta^* \) with some confidence level (e.g., 95%).

Without diving too deeply into Frequentist inference, we identify some qualities that raise some concerns in application. Firstly, Frequentist ideology suggests the existence of some true constant parameter \( \theta^* \) that objectively describes the distribution of the data. Secondly, Frequentist inference relies on the notion of confidence intervals, which lack axiomatic interpretations in probability calculus. Additionally, the Frequentist approach reduces our analysis to a cross-sectional snapshot of an unknown fixed parameter. Bayesian inference addresses these conccerns by treating the parameter as a random variable that is informed by the observed data and by making claims about the distribution of the parameter itself that can be directly derived from the axioms of probability. Furthermore, we will discuss how iterative learning properties can help capture adaptations to our inference that have intuitive extensions to temporal data. This branch of ideology follows the structure of Thomas Bayes' concept of a posterior distribution that is dependent on the likelihood of the observed data and some prior distribution that encodes our beliefs about the parameter before observing the data. The relationship is described by Bayes' theorem, which states that the posterior distribution is proportional to the product of the likelihood and the prior distribution.

\[ \text{Posterior} = \frac{\text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior}}{\text{Marginal}} \] \[ p(\theta | x) = \frac{f(x | \theta) p(\theta)}{p(x)} \] The above relationship includes a normalizing constant \( p(x) \) that ensures the posterior distribution integrates to 1, making it a valid probability distribution. We often call this the marginal distribution.

The result of Bayesian inference is a credible interval \( C(x) \) such that \( P(\theta \in C(x) | x) = 1 - \alpha \), where \( \alpha \) is the desired level of credibility (e.g., 0.05 for a 95% credible interval). In addition, Bayesian inference invites iterative learning to the statistical model. By replacing the prior distribution with the most recent posterior distribution, we obtain an adaptive model that reflects our beliefs as new data affirms or contradicts our existing model. A continuously updating model intuitively captures the learning process of the parameter as a random variable that can adapt over time, contrasting the Frequentist interpretation of a cross-sectional snapshot of a fixed parameter.
